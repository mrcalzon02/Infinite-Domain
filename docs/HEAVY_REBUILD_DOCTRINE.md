# Infinite Domain — Peak-Quality Quest Structure Schematic Rebuild Standard

**Repository status:** AUTHORITATIVE HEAVY REBUILD DOCTRINE  
**Applies to:** Old World quest-location schematic reconstruction and quality revision  
**Execution rule:** Read and obey this doctrine before performing any heavy schematic rebuild pass.

## Purpose

This document establishes the mandatory rebuilding process for existing **Infinite Domain Old World quest-location schematics**.

The current rough structures are to be treated as **functional sketches, spatial notes, and placeholders**, not finished architecture.

A rough structure may establish:

- approximate purpose;
- approximate footprint;
- approximate number of floors;
- broad room relationships;
- existing quest integration;
- structure ID;
- world-generation intent;
- loot-table associations;
- narrative affiliation;
- required evidence;
- required encounter mechanics.

It does **not** establish an obligation to preserve crude geometry.

If an existing structure resembles something constructed at an elementary-school level—large rectangular rooms, flat walls, arbitrary windows, oversized empty interiors, hallways without purpose, stairs that exist only because stairs seemed necessary, random decorative blocks, meaningless symmetry, or an exterior that communicates nothing beyond "building"—those elements are not heritage to be preserved.

**Rebuild them.**

The objective is to transform every qualifying quest location into a structure that could plausibly be mistaken for the work of an expert Minecraft environment-building team.

The desired standard is not:

> "The old structure, but prettier."

The desired standard is:

> **A believable real-world institution translated into Minecraft, shaped by a recognizable corporate or organizational design language, modified by the historical events of Infinite Domain, and finally transformed into a carefully staged exploration, combat, loot, and narrative environment.**

---

# I. Governing Development Rule

The rebuilding process must continue to obey the project's larger development rule:

**Existence, integration, and mechanical correctness come before architectural polish.**

Therefore each structure has two separate forms of completion.

### Functional status

A structure may be:

- indexed;
- static implemented;
- registered;
- quest integrated;
- loot integrated;
- mechanically validated;
- worldgen ready;
- runtime validated.

### Quality status

Separately, it may be:

- placeholder;
- rough;
- rebuild required;
- architectural pass;
- interior pass;
- narrative pass;
- encounter pass;
- detailing pass;
- schematic revision complete;
- peak-quality approved.

A mechanically working structure is not automatically an architecturally finished structure.

A beautiful structure with broken quest logic is also not finished.

Both tracks must eventually converge.

---

# II. Absolute Rebuild Principle

## Treat the existing schematic as a program diagram

Do not begin by replacing cobblestone with better cobblestone.

Do not begin by adding trapdoors.

Do not begin by randomly indenting walls.

Do not begin by adding pipes to the ceiling.

Do not begin by throwing decorative blocks onto the facade.

First determine:

**What is this building?**

Then determine:

**How would a real building performing this function actually work?**

Then determine:

**How would this particular organization have built it?**

Then determine:

**What happened here during the fall?**

Then determine:

**How does the player experience that history?**

Only after those questions have answers should detailed block placement begin.

# III. Mandatory Phase 0 — Existing Structure Audit

Before altering a schematic, create a rebuild dossier.

Record:

### Identity

- OWS ID or equivalent structure ID.
- Registry ID.
- Structure name.
- Owning or affiliated organization.
- Era or narrative period.
- Rarity.
- Worldgen category.
- Exploration quest association.
- Required evidence item.
- Existing loot-table references.
- Existing proof mechanics.
- Existing advancement or FTB quest detection.
- Existing structure processor or placement requirements.

### Existing geometry

Record:

- footprint;
- height;
- floor count;
- major entrances;
- major rooms;
- underground areas;
- roof access;
- obvious intended machinery;
- existing chest locations;
- existing spawners;
- quest-object locations.

### Existing value

Explicitly identify which pieces are worth retaining.

A rough schematic may contain one genuinely good idea buried inside fifty bad ones.

Preserve the good idea.

Examples:

- an interesting collapsed atrium;
- a useful underground laboratory;
- an unusual loading dock;
- a recognizable tower;
- a good central reactor hall;
- an effective quarantine chamber;
- a useful roof silhouette;
- a strong final evidence room.

Everything else remains negotiable.

Every structure must have a **real institutional purpose**.

"Laboratory" is insufficient.

Define what kind.

- satellite integration facility;
- emergency epidemiological command center.

A legitimate facility usually contains far more than its dramatic centerpiece.

A laboratory requires some combination of:

- staff access;
- laboratories;
- preparation rooms;
- sample storage;
- washing/decontamination;
- chemical storage;
- utilities;
- mechanical plant;
- toilets;
- changing areas;
- break areas;
- records;
- loading;
- waste handling;
- emergency equipment;
- maintenance access;
- circulation.

A warehouse requires:

- truck approach;
- loading docks;
- receiving;
- inspection;
- staging;
- bulk storage;
- picking;
- packing;
- dispatch;
- offices;
- staff support;
- maintenance;
- charging/fuel areas;
- utility rooms;
- waste;
- fire egress.

A hospital requires:

- entrance;
- waiting;
- admissions;
- treatment;
- diagnostics;
- wards;
- nursing;
- pharmacy;
- sterile processing;
- staff spaces;
- service corridors;
- utilities;
- storage;
- loading;
- emergency systems.

The Minecraft building does not need every room found in reality.

It must contain **enough of them that its existence makes sense**.

This is mandatory for uncommon, rare, landmark, and quest-critical structures.

# VI. Translating Reality Into Minecraft's One-Meter Grid

Minecraft's approximate one-block-per-meter spatial scale requires deliberate exaggeration.

A real wall may be twenty centimeters thick.

Minecraft cannot represent that properly.

Therefore realism must be achieved through **proportional translation rather than literal dimensional copying**.

## Typical floor heights

Use approximately:

- cramped utility spaces: 3–4 blocks clear;
- ordinary offices: 4 blocks clear;
- laboratories: 4–5 blocks clear;
- hospitals/institutional spaces: 4–5 blocks clear;
- prestige lobbies: 6–10 blocks;
- warehouses: 7–14 blocks;
- industrial machine halls: 8–18 blocks;
- turbine/reactor/aerospace halls: potentially 15–30+ blocks.

Include floor/ceiling structure above that clear height.

A four-block room should not mean four blocks from floor surface to the next floor surface.

Structural slabs, suspended ceilings, ducts, service cavities, or roof construction need volume.

## Wall thickness

Use one-block walls only where appropriate for ordinary internal partitions.

Important exterior or industrial walls should frequently be:

- 2 blocks thick;
- locally 3+ blocks thick;
- layered with columns;
- structural framing;
- service cavities;
- cladding;
- buttresses;
- pilasters;
- exterior equipment.

This creates architectural depth rather than cardboard buildings.

## Corridors

Typical useful Minecraft widths:

- restricted maintenance passage: 1–2 blocks;
- staff corridor: 2 blocks;
- normal institutional corridor: 3 blocks;
- high-traffic hospital/public corridor: 3–5 blocks;
- industrial service route: 4–8 blocks.

Do not make every corridor three blocks wide.

Function determines width.

## Doors and openings

Use:

- single doors for minor offices;
- double doors for public/clinical spaces;
- wider openings for labs and equipment movement;
- substantial loading doors for industrial spaces;
- security vestibules where appropriate.

A forklift-sized machine should not enter through the same doorway as an office worker.

## Stairs

Major stairs should feel intentionally built.

Avoid endless one-block-wide staircases whenever the institution would realistically require substantial circulation.

Use:

- landings;
- stairwells;
- railings;
- fire doors;
- structural walls;
- lighting;
- occasional intermediate floors.

Industrial stairs may be narrower.

Public institutional stairs should generally be wider.

---

# VII. Massing Before Decoration

Rebuild the building at the largest scale first.

The silhouette should communicate function from a distance.

A player approaching the structure should begin forming a hypothesis before reading a sign.

### Agricultural biotechnology

Greenhouse masses, cultivation towers, processing annexes, nutrient tanks.

### Heavy industry

Large-span halls, stacks, cranes, loading yards, gantries.

### Logistics

Long warehouse forms, truck bays, containers, staging yards.

### Medicine

Interconnected wings, patient towers, ambulance access, institutional entrances.

### Energy

Massive reinforced volumes, switching yards, stacks, cooling equipment, cable infrastructure.

### Data infrastructure

Secure window-poor masses, generator yards, cooling equipment, communications infrastructure.

### Aerospace

Huge clear-span buildings, hangars, tracking equipment, control buildings, service towers.

A rectangular box should survive the massing pass only when a rectangular box genuinely suits the building—and even then its surrounding infrastructure must explain it.

---

# VIII. Structural Logic

Buildings should appear capable of standing up.

Use visible or implied:

- column grids;
- load-bearing walls;
- beams;
- trusses;
- roof framing;
- foundations;
- service cores;
- structural bays.

Large open spans require different construction from offices.

A huge warehouse ceiling resting magically upon twenty-meter unsupported flat slabs is unacceptable.

A large hall should show:

- trusses;
- beams;
- support columns;
- roof ribs;
- suspended services;
- catwalks where useful.

The structure does not need to be engineering-simulation accurate.

It needs to look as though someone understood engineering.

---

# IX. Circulation Is Architecture

One of the biggest differences between amateur and professional-looking Minecraft architecture is **circulation**.

Rooms must relate logically.

Ask:

How does a visitor enter?

How does an employee enter?

How does freight enter?

How does waste leave?

How do technicians reach machinery?

How are secure areas separated?

How does someone reach another floor?

Where are emergency exits?

Where does the player naturally go?

Do not build:

**door → enormous room → door → enormous room → treasure room.**

Instead create sequences.

For example:

**public lobby → access-control point → staff corridor → observation lab → sample preparation → controlled corridor → containment area → failed emergency barrier → research archive.**

The player's exploration route can therefore mirror how the building itself operated.

---

# X. Front-of-House and Back-of-House

High-quality buildings need both.

Front-of-house architecture shows what the organization wanted the public to see.

Back-of-house architecture shows how the organization actually operated.

This distinction is particularly important for Infinite Domain.

An Aevum lobby may be magnificent.

Behind it are:

- sterile supply rooms;
- hazardous waste;
- maintenance corridors;
- biological storage;
- machinery;
- freight elevators;
- staff lockers;
- emergency generators.

A VCF visitor center may show clean sustainable agriculture.

Behind it are thousands of liters of nutrient solution and industrial biological processing.

A Blackglass office may look like an expensive technology company.

Below it are hardened server vaults.

Back-of-house spaces are among the strongest tools available for making a structure believable.

---

# XI. Five Required Scales of Detail

Peak-quality structures must work at five visual scales.

## 1. Skyline scale

Recognizable shape at a distance.

## 2. Building scale

Entrances, wings, setbacks, roof forms, service areas, structural rhythm.

## 3. Facade scale

Windows, columns, cladding transitions, canopies, loading doors, vents, equipment.

## 4. Room scale

Ceilings, floors, lighting, machinery, furniture, partitions, storage.

## 5. Prop scale

Desks, cabinets, tools, screens, samples, pallets, bins, damaged equipment, signs.

Do not compensate for weak macro architecture with thousands of trapdoors.

Micro-detail cannot save a bad building.

---

# XII. Corporate Architectural Languages

Corporate identity must alter the **architecture itself**, not merely the logo or palette.

A player should eventually recognize the organization from form and machinery before seeing its name.

---

## VCF — Verdant Continuum Foods

VCF should communicate **industrial abundance disguised as clean biological optimism**.

### Architectural language

Use:

- bright industrial interiors;
- controlled greenhouse spaces;
- large windows where biologically appropriate;
- cultivation halls;
- modular growing chambers;
- stainless or sanitary surfaces;
- nutrient plumbing;
- drainage;
- laboratory observation windows;
- clean service corridors;
- processing rooms;
- packaging areas.

VCF sites should show that fungal agriculture was not weird fringe science.

It was an astonishingly successful global food industry.

Early facilities can be beautiful.

Efficient.

Clean.

Optimistic.

Later crisis modifications introduce:

- quarantine plastic/barriers;
- replacement filters;
- improvised sealing;
- abandoned protective equipment;
- blocked ducts;
- burned grow chambers;
- contradictory contamination signs.

The horror is stronger because the original system was impressive.

---

## Atlas Kinetic Industries

Atlas represents **industrial automation**.

Its identity must come primarily through machinery.

Use:

- machine halls;
- modular production bays;
- conveyors;
- crushers;
- mixers;
- presses;
- assembly lines;
- industrial elevators;
- gantries;
- catwalks;
- overhead services;
- large maintenance doors;
- parts storage;
- tool cages;
- maintenance pits.

Atlas architecture should be practical.

Buildings are designed around machines rather than around people.

The player learning Create should begin to recognize the mechanical grammar of these facilities.

Do not turn Atlas into generic steampunk decoration.

Machines must appear to perform actual industrial processes.

Follow material flow:

**input → processing → intermediate storage → additional processing → quality control → finished output → shipping.**

---

## PolyCore Advanced Materials

PolyCore represents **materials science and increasingly desperate containment engineering**.

Use:

- technical laboratories;
- test cells;
- exposure chambers;
- sample storage;
- material racks;
- chemical rooms;
- pressure/testing rigs;
- membrane testing;
- filter testing;
- progressively modified containment rooms.

Earlier PolyCore sites should be confident and orderly.

Later facilities should visually demonstrate experimentation accelerating beyond normal institutional control.

One corridor may contain several generations of door seals.

One chamber may have an original polymer barrier followed by a steel retrofit.

Replacement components may pile up beside failed components.

Late PolyCore buildings should look like brilliant engineers slowly running out of materials that work.

That story must be visible without text.

---

## Pleroma Distribution Group

Pleroma represents **logistical scale**.

Use:

- recognizable branded freight containers;
- loading docks;
- pallet storage;
- conveyor sorting;
- cold-storage rooms;
- route numbers;
- loading lanes;
- mezzanine offices;
- shipping control;
- forklift routes;
- packaging;
- truck or rail staging;
- huge storage volumes.

The environment should clearly demonstrate that Pleroma could move almost anything almost anywhere.

That same accomplishment later becomes horrifying when the player understands what was being transported.

Pleroma facilities should emphasize **movement**.

Even ruined buildings should show where goods came from, where they went, and how they moved through the facility.

---

## Aevum Therapeutics

Aevum represents **medical prestige and humanity's extraordinary success against injury, disease, and aging**.

Do not make Aevum evil-looking.

That weakens the story.

Important Aevum facilities should often be among the most beautiful Old World structures.

Use:

- architectural glass;
- high-quality clinical interiors;
- comfortable patient areas;
- gardens or atriums where appropriate;
- rehabilitation areas;
- advanced treatment suites;
- surgery;
- imaging;
- clinical laboratories;
- pharmaceutical preparation;
- secure biological research.

Then contrast these areas against escalating emergency adaptations.

Beds migrate into corridors.

Supply caches accumulate.

Security doors appear in places never designed for them.

Research wings become quarantine wings.

Emergency posters appear beside advertisements promising longer, healthier lives.

The tragedy is that Aevum's breakthrough **worked**.

---

## Helion Energy Systems

Helion represents **heavy energy infrastructure and civilization-scale power**.

Use:

- reinforced concrete;
- large machinery;
- turbine halls;
- transformer yards;
- substations;
- cable galleries;
- pipe racks;
- coolant infrastructure;
- control rooms;
- maintenance cranes;
- pump rooms;
- emergency generators;
- redundant systems;
- protected operator spaces.

Failure should proceed through believable infrastructure.

Do not simply explode the center of the building.

Show:

- failed pumps;
- damaged seals;
- flooded cable areas;
- auxiliary systems offline;
- temporary bypasses;
- emergency shutdown work;
- abandoned repair equipment.

A Helion ruin should tell the story of engineers fighting cascading system failure.

---

## Blackglass Information Systems

Blackglass represents **information, encryption, communications, finance, and the surviving memory of the Old World**.

Use:

- controlled entrances;
- security vestibules;
- secure stair cores;
- limited glazing in hardened sections;
- server rooms;
- cable infrastructure;
- backup power;
- cooling plants;
- network operations centers;
- archive rooms;
- protected basement spaces;
- communications arrays.

Blackglass structures should contain obvious **security depth**.

Public office areas can exist.

The important information should not sit behind one wooden door.

Use progressively restricted layers.

Later Darknet progression may give the player reasons to return to areas that were inaccessible or uninterpretable earlier.

Architecture should support this naturally.

---

## Asterion Orbital Industries

Asterion represents **aerospace scale, precision, and humanity reaching beyond Earth**.

Use:

- huge high bays;
- assembly floors;
- overhead cranes;
- gantries;
- clean integration spaces;
- engineering offices;
- control rooms;
- telemetry;
- satellite equipment;
- fueling/service areas where applicable;
- tracking arrays;
- launch support;
- large secured logistics.

Asterion architecture should feel expensive and exceptionally capable.

Avoid turning it into fantasy science-fiction architecture.

It should look like the advanced descendant of real aerospace engineering.

The extraordinary things should feel technologically earned.

# XIII. Continuity — Special Architectural Doctrine

Continuity must never be treated as "Corporation Number Nine."

Continuity is a coalition.

A network.

A group of scientists, engineers, researchers, analysts, epidemiologists, and specialists who gradually realized that the world's fundamental model of the disaster was wrong.

Their architecture therefore communicates something unique:

**they usually inherited buildings rather than commissioning them.**

A Continuity facility might originally have been:

- a university laboratory;
- a VCF satellite office;
- a PolyCore research wing;
- a government emergency center;
- a communications station;
- a basement beneath a hospital;
- an abandoned warehouse;
- a military research bunker;
- an atmospheric monitoring site.

Then Continuity took it over.

## Continuity visual language

Use visible layers of repurposing.

A corporate conference room becomes a mapping room.

A chemistry laboratory contains atmospheric models.

A basement server room contains copied corporate records.

An employee cafeteria contains field cots.

A loading bay contains generators and water storage.

A laboratory freezer sits beside emergency food.

A former executive office becomes a radio room.

A storage area becomes an airlock.

Continuity rooms should contain evidence acquired from multiple institutions.

VCF reports.

PolyCore testing.

Aevum clinical findings.

Atmospheric maps.

Government orders.

Military projections.

Pleroma shipping information.

Blackglass data.

Continuity's power came from **connecting things that everyone else viewed separately**.

The building should physically communicate this.

---

# XIV. Continuity Survivor-Bunker Doctrine

Later Continuity structures should become some of the most emotionally powerful locations in the pack.

These people understood sooner than most that civilization might not survive.

Their facilities therefore transition from:

**research center**

into:

**emergency research center**

into:

**survival installation**

into:

**last scientific outpost.**

Include sensible survival infrastructure.

Examples:

- food stores;
- water reserves;
- filtration;
- spare filters;
- improvised sleeping quarters;
- cots;
- medical supplies;
- batteries;
- generators;
- fuel storage;
- communications;
- repair benches;
- spare clothing;
- protective equipment;
- seed stores where appropriate;
- maps;
- physical archives;
- redundant paper records;
- sanitation;
- waste handling;
- emergency exits.

Do not make Continuity look militaristically omnipotent.

They were not a secret super-government with infinite resources.

They were intelligent people improvising with what they could acquire.

Their structures should sometimes contain three different corporate equipment standards connected together because **they used whatever still worked**.

That gives them an architectural identity despite having no standardized architecture.

---

# XV. Heroism Without Sanitization

Continuity should be sympathetic without becoming absurdly perfect.

They were correct about major parts of the catastrophe.

That does not mean every Continuity decision was correct.

Their structures may show:

- arguments;
- abandoned models;
- contradictory projections;
- failed experiments;
- exhaustion;
- triage;
- resource shortages;
- desperate repairs;
- increasingly bleak survival planning.

What distinguishes them is not infallibility.

It is that they were willing to follow the evidence farther than other institutions.

Their last facilities should feel like places inhabited by people who knew exactly how bad the situation had become and continued working anyway.

---

# XVI. Collapse-State Layering

Every quest-grade ruin should contain multiple historical layers.

Never build an intact building and then randomly punch holes in it.

Instead identify its history.

## Layer 1 — Normal operation

What did the facility look like when the world worked?

Preserve enough of this layer that its original purpose remains obvious.

## Layer 2 — Early anomaly

Small signs of trouble.

Replacement components.

Warning signs.

Temporary inspections.

Unexpected maintenance.

Restricted areas.

## Layer 3 — Crisis retrofit

Now the building begins changing.

Barricades.

Quarantine zones.

Temporary filtration.

Emergency power.

Improvised storage.

Military or government intervention.

Beds in offices.

Blocked doors.

Additional barriers.

## Layer 4 — Final operation

The facility is surviving rather than functioning normally.

Supplies diminish.

Rooms are repurposed.

Repairs become crude.

Some systems are abandoned to save others.

Access routes change.

Personnel concentrate in defensible or survivable sections.

## Layer 5 — Collapse

Determine how this particular location failed.

Not every structure suffered the same disaster.

Possible causes include:

- evacuation;
- biological contamination;
- infrastructure failure;
- fire;
- flooding;
- grid failure;
- structural damage;
- fighting;
- starvation;
- abandonment;
- sterilization operations;
- regional bombardment.

## Layer 6 — Centuries of decay

Finally apply the modern wasteland state.

Weather.

Collapsed roofs.

Water.

Vegetation.

Fungal occupation.

Sediment.

Rust.

Broken glass.

Missing structural sections.

But retain enough evidence to reconstruct Layers 1–5.

---

# XVII. Environmental Storytelling Requirement

Every quest-grade location must communicate **at least one major narrative fact without requiring the player to read a book**.

Preferably several.

Examples:

A supposedly sealed room has contamination around the air vent.

Replacement gaskets surround a failed pump.

A quarantine barrier has contamination on both sides.

A Pleroma train or warehouse contains sealed VCF agricultural products.

PolyCore test chambers contain increasingly exotic barrier materials.

A hospital advertises life-extension treatment while emergency ration notices cover the walls.

A Helion control room shows repeated attempts to bypass failed coolant support systems.

A Continuity bunker contains atmospheric maps beside reports from several unrelated corporations.

Books and recordings then explain what the player is already beginning to suspect.

The text should confirm and deepen environmental evidence rather than substitute for it.

---

# XVIII. Signs, Wayfinding, and Institutional Language

Real buildings label things.

Use signage extensively.

Examples include:

- departments;
- room numbers;
- hazard levels;
- loading bays;
- restricted areas;
- evacuation routes;
- cultivation zones;
- treatment wings;
- machine identifiers;
- power systems;
- access levels.

Signage is also an inexpensive way to make large facilities legible to players.

Corporate signage should initially sound professional.

Crisis signage becomes more direct.

Late emergency signage becomes brutally simple.

The visual change in signage itself can become part of the timeline.

---

# XIX. Encounter Architecture

These structures are quest locations.

They therefore need deliberate combat design.

Vanilla monster spawners may be used, but they must be integrated into the level rather than scattered randomly.

## Recommended encounter layers

### Threshold encounter

A modest encounter near or shortly after entry establishes that the structure is dangerous.

### Interior pressure encounter

A second encounter controls a useful intersection, stairwell, collapsed section, industrial room, or loot area.

### Deep-site encounter

A harder encounter protects an important research, archive, utility, or evidence zone.

### Optional risk/reward encounter

An especially dangerous side area may contain better practical loot without blocking mandatory progression.

Not every structure needs all four.

---

# XX. Spawner Placement Rules

Spawner placement must have spatial logic.

Good locations include:

- contaminated rooms;
- collapsed basements;
- maintenance tunnels;
- flooded sublevels;
- sealed quarantine chambers;
- dark warehouse stacks;
- abandoned utility areas;
- fungal overgrowth zones;
- ruined dormitories;
- inaccessible corners exposed by collapse.

Use only appropriate **vanilla creatures** unless another specification explicitly authorizes otherwise.

Potential choices include:

- zombies;
- skeletons;
- spiders;
- cave spiders;
- drowned in genuinely flooded environments;
- husks where the biome/environment supports them;
- silverfish in highly specific damaged structural areas.

Avoid treating mob selection as random variety.

The environment should justify the creature.

### Protect quest-critical rooms

Do not casually use explosive encounters around irreplaceable quest evidence.

The player should not lose progression because combat destroyed the only proof item.

### Player agency

Whenever practical, allow players to:

- locate the source of an encounter;
- fight toward it;
- disable or control it;
- choose whether optional loot is worth the risk.

Spawner combat should create exploration pressure rather than infinite annoyance.

---

# XXI. Encounter Density

Do not saturate every room with mobs.

As a starting guideline rather than an inflexible quota:

### Small site

Approximately 1–2 meaningful spawner encounters.

### Medium site

Approximately 2–5.

### Large quest site

Approximately 4–8 distributed encounter sources.

### Landmark

Design encounters by zones rather than simply increasing raw numbers.

Large structures should rely on **spatial encounter progression**, not a wall of twenty spawners.

Performance remains relevant.

---

# XXII. Loot Architecture

Loot is part of the facility's function.

Never place every chest in a single treasure room unless that particular room actually functioned as secure storage.

Establish loot classes.

## Incidental loot

Desks.

Lockers.

Break rooms.

Maintenance carts.

Minor storage.

## Operational loot

Workshops.

Supply rooms.

Laboratories.

Machine areas.

Warehouses.

## Secured corporate loot

Controlled laboratories.

Executive archives.

Pharmacies.

Technical storage.

Security rooms.

High-value material cages.

## Quest evidence

The structure-specific proof item.

These categories should occupy different spaces.

---

# XXIII. Existing Loot Tables Are Authoritative

Corporate loot families and their loot tables already exist.

Therefore:

**DO NOT invent replacement loot tables merely because a schematic is being rebuilt.**

Codex must first locate the existing table associated with the organization and structure family.

Use the existing:

- VCF;
- Atlas;
- PolyCore;
- Pleroma;
- Aevum;
- Helion;
- Blackglass;
- Asterion;
- Continuity

loot systems wherever they already exist.

Do not manually hard-code random chest contents that duplicate an existing table.

If the expected table cannot be found, report the missing reference.

Do not silently fabricate a new naming convention.

---

# XXIV. Corporate Loot Should Reflect Function

Physical placement should still distinguish the corporations.

### VCF

Food, seeds, cultivation materials, biological-processing supplies, agricultural chemicals, machinery components.

### Atlas

Machine parts, industrial components, tooling, mechanical materials, automation-related supplies.

### PolyCore

Advanced materials, polymers, laboratory chemicals, filters, gaskets, test components.

### Pleroma

General cargo, food distribution, logistics supplies, packaged goods, freight materials.

### Aevum

Medical supplies, clinical materials, pharmaceuticals where permitted by the existing tables, research supplies.

### Helion

Electrical components, technical hardware, metals, power-system materials, nuclear-related components where already established.

### Blackglass

Electronic components, communications equipment, secure records, data-related items.

### Asterion

Precision components, aerospace materials, technical equipment, orbital research items.

### Continuity

Continuity is again different.

Its loot should plausibly mix:

- emergency survival supplies;
- scientific equipment;
- medical supplies;
- electronics;
- spare parts;
- records;
- food;
- filtration;
- tools;
- scavenged or requisitioned equipment from multiple corporate families.

The mixture itself tells the story.

---

# XXV. Guaranteed Quest Evidence

Every structure used to satisfy a specific Exploration objective must maintain a **guaranteed proof path**.

The player must actually search the structure.

The proof should therefore usually be located beyond some meaningful exploration.

However it must not depend purely upon low-probability random loot.

Examples include:

- unique archive fragment;
- corporate record;
- research badge;
- named cartridge;
- Continuity document;
- atmospheric model;
- orbital archive;
- facility-specific evidence token.

The evidence location should be narratively appropriate.

Do not put a facility's most important research record in a random bathroom chest.

Potential locations include:

- secure archive;
- lead researcher's office;
- control room;
- emergency records room;
- evidence locker;
- bunker command space;
- sealed laboratory;
- final operating position.

---

# XXVI. Loot and Combat Must Interact

Important loot should sometimes require risk.

Optional secure storage can be placed behind harder encounters.

Conversely, do not make every chest a combat arena.

Good exploration rhythm alternates:

**tension → discovery → reward → quiet investigation → rising danger → major discovery.**

Minecraft structures become exhausting when every doorway initiates combat.

They become boring when nothing resists the player.

Build rhythm.

---

# XXVII. Facade Quality Standard

A finished facade must have **depth**, not just color variation.

Use combinations of:

- structural bays;
- vertical supports;
- window recesses;
- entrance projections;
- service volumes;
- loading docks;
- canopies;
- roof parapets;
- drainage;
- utility runs;
- cladding transitions;
- emergency stairs;
- vents;
- rooftop equipment.

Large uninterrupted flat walls should exist only where the real building purpose justifies them.

Warehouse walls may be broad.

They should still show structural rhythm, expansion joints, loading functions, vents, drains, service doors, or panel divisions.

---

# XXVIII. Window Standard

Stop using windows as evenly spaced decorative holes.

Windows must correspond to interior function.

Offices may receive regular glazing.

Laboratories may have limited or controlled glazing.

Warehouses may have high strip windows.

Data centers may have almost none.

A secure Blackglass basement should not have cheerful floor-to-ceiling windows because the facade needed variation.

Interior planning and facade planning must agree.

---

# XXIX. Roof Standard

The roof is a fifth facade.

Never finish a major structure with a giant empty flat rectangle.

Use appropriate:

- mechanical plant;
- vents;
- exhausts;
- cooling;
- access housings;
- skylights;
- drainage;
- antennae;
- service paths;
- greenhouse systems;
- solar or technical systems where established;
- structural changes;
- damaged areas.

Roof equipment should connect conceptually to equipment below.

Do not create a random forest of pipes.

---

# XXX. Interior Quality Standard

Every significant room needs three answers:

1. What was this room for?
2. What equipment did that require?
3. What happened to it?

Furniture should not exist simply to fill space.

A laboratory should contain laboratory workflow.

A machine room should contain machines and service clearance.

A warehouse should contain goods movement.

An office should contain workstations, storage, and circulation.

A control room should face or monitor something.

A security desk should control an actual access route.

---

# XXXI. Ceiling and Utility Standard

Amateur builds frequently detail the walls and forget the ceiling.

Do not.

Use:

- lighting;
- beams;
- ducts;
- cable trays;
- sprinkler analogues where appropriate;
- pipes;
- suspended equipment;
- ceiling panels;
- industrial trusses;
- maintenance access.

Again, utilities must have logic.

A pipe should appear to go somewhere.

A ventilation trunk should serve rooms.

Electrical infrastructure should connect machines, control areas, and power spaces.

Decorative nonsense is not technical detail.

---

# XXXII. Service Infrastructure

Large facilities need visible support systems.

Depending upon purpose include:

- power;
- water;
- drainage;
- ventilation;
- cooling;
- heating;
- waste;
- chemical delivery;
- data;
- compressed systems;
- emergency power;
- loading.

A high-quality build should make the player think:

> "I can see how this place worked."

That is the target.

---

# XXXIII. Controlled Imperfection

Real buildings are not perfectly symmetric.

Use asymmetry deliberately.

An annex may have been added later.

A loading area may distort one side.

Mechanical equipment may occupy a roof corner.

A stair core may project beyond the facade.

A hospital may have an older wing and newer wing.

A crisis retrofit may block a previously elegant hallway.

This produces history.

Do not replace simplistic symmetry with random asymmetry.

Every irregularity should have a plausible reason.

---

# XXXIV. Damage Must Follow Physics

Do not use random-air-block destruction.

Structural damage should consider:

- gravity;
- load paths;
- fire direction;
- explosion source;
- water entry;
- roof collapse;
- falling debris;
- weakened supports.

If a floor has collapsed, show where debris went.

If a wall was breached, show material around the breach.

If fire occurred, show progression and secondary damage.

If water entered, lower areas should usually suffer more.

A ruin should still obey cause and effect.

---

# XXXV. Avoid Over-Ruining

The Old World must remain legible.

If every building is reduced to 40% random rubble, environmental storytelling disappears.

Preserve enough intact material for the player to understand:

- what the building was;
- who operated it;
- how it worked;
- what changed;
- how it failed.

Decay is not the content.

**The civilization beneath the decay is the content.**

---

# XXXVI. Anti-"Minecraft Builder Slop" Rules

The following do **not** qualify as a rebuild:

- palette swapping;
- adding stairs around windows;
- putting trapdoors everywhere;
- adding random wall indentations;
- excessive gradients;
- covering everything with vines;
- random pipes;
- arbitrary barrels;
- adding five decorative roof chimneys;
- building giant empty atriums;
- adding meaningless catwalks;
- using random block noise as "weathering";
- replacing every smooth surface with visual clutter.

Detail must explain architecture, operation, history, or damage.

If a detail explains nothing, question why it exists.

---

# XXXVII. Room Legitimacy Rule

A finished quest building should contain almost no significant spaces for which the answer to:

> "What was this room used for?"

is:

> "Nothing."

An empty room can exist.

But it should be empty for a reason:

- evacuated;
- cleared;
- warehouse stock removed;
- stripped for equipment;
- converted during emergency operations;
- collapsed;
- intentionally sterile.

Meaningless voids are unacceptable.

---

# XXXVIII. Architectural Variety Without Chaos

Each building should develop a controlled material hierarchy.

Typically use:

### Primary structural family

The blocks defining most construction.

### Secondary architectural family

Cladding, interior partitions, additions.

### Technical family

Machinery, metalwork, utilities.

### Corporate accent family

Signage and selected institutional identity.

### Decay family

Damage, staining, contamination, repairs.

Do not use thirty block types merely because they exist.

Professional builds often look coherent because the palette is disciplined.

---

# XXXIX. Site Design Beyond the Walls

Whenever schematic bounds permit, the facility should include its immediate operational environment.

Examples:

- parking;
- loading;
- service roads;
- security fencing;
- utility yards;
- drainage;
- tanks;
- dumpsters;
- substations;
- guard points;
- courtyards;
- greenhouses;
- rail spurs;
- containers;
- ambulance approach;
- communications equipment.

A sophisticated building sitting alone in a square field often still looks like a Minecraft build.

A believable site feels like a piece of a world.

---

# XL. Expansion of Rough Structures

Expansion is explicitly permitted when required to make the building believable.

Do not preserve an implausibly small structure simply because the placeholder was small.

The one-meter block grid naturally forces certain buildings to grow.

A real facility whose corridors, walls, stairs, service systems, and structural layers occupy fractions of meters may require substantially more Minecraft volume.

Therefore enlargement is expected when translating:

- laboratories;
- hospitals;
- industrial plants;
- logistics centers;
- data centers;
- aerospace structures.

Expansion must remain proportional to intended rarity and worldgen cost.

Not every warehouse needs to become a megastructure.

---

# XLI. Player Navigation

Realism must not produce an incomprehensible maze.

Use environmental wayfinding.

Give major areas recognizable visual identities.

Examples:

- colored departmental stripes;
- room numbering;
- machinery types;
- lighting changes;
- signs;
- floor materials;
- architectural transitions;
- landmark rooms.

Players should be allowed to become temporarily lost.

They should not become unable to understand the building.

---

# XLII. Exploration Loops

Whenever architecture allows, avoid forcing every quest location into one linear corridor.

Use:

- alternate routes;
- loops;
- balconies;
- service corridors;
- breached shortcuts;
- stair connections;
- roof access;
- optional side areas.

The building should feel like a place, not a dungeon hallway disguised as a workplace.

However the quest path can still use spatial gating to gradually expose important narrative areas.

---

# XLIII. Sightline Design

Use sightlines to create anticipation.

Examples:

The player sees the enormous machine hall through glass before finding the entrance.

A collapsed balcony reveals a lower containment level.

A sealed archive is visible behind reinforced glazing.

A distant emergency light indicates another route.

The final Continuity command area may be visible from an upper observation corridor long before it is reachable.

This turns architecture into exploration design.

---

# XLIV. Hero Spaces

Every medium or larger quest structure should have one or more memorable spaces.

Examples:

### VCF

A towering vertical cultivation chamber.

### Atlas

A complete automated production hall.

### PolyCore

A multi-generation containment test chamber.

### Pleroma

A massive automated sorting hall.

### Aevum

A regenerative-treatment atrium.

### Helion

A turbine or reactor-support hall.

### Blackglass

A secure central network/archive chamber.

### Asterion

A spacecraft/satellite integration bay.

### Continuity

A final analysis room covered in maps, cross-corporate evidence, communications, and survival infrastructure.

These spaces become visual memories attached to the quest.

---

# XLV. Final-Pass Microdetail

Only after architecture, circulation, rooms, encounters, loot, and narrative are correct should micro-detail begin.

Then add:

- cables;
- conduit;
- tools;
- labels;
- small storage;
- paperwork analogues;
- emergency lamps;
- broken furnishings;
- floor markings;
- maintenance equipment;
- ceiling systems;
- local damage;
- debris.

This is the final 10–15% of the build.

It must never be used to disguise failure in the first 85%.

---

# XLVI. Rebuild Execution Sequence

For every schematic, Codex should follow this order.

## Pass 1 — Audit

Understand the existing structure and mechanics.

## Pass 2 — Functional definition

Define exactly what real institution this represents.

## Pass 3 — Precedent research

Study real-world examples.

## Pass 4 — Program and adjacency

Determine rooms, support spaces, and circulation.

## Pass 5 — Scale translation

Expand to believable Minecraft dimensions.

## Pass 6 — Massing

Rebuild silhouette and building volumes.

## Pass 7 — Structural system

Add columns, spans, floors, roof structure.

## Pass 8 — Circulation

Build entrances, corridors, stairs, service routes.

## Pass 9 — Exterior architecture

Develop facades and site infrastructure.

## Pass 10 — Interior architecture

Construct walls, ceilings, floors, room identities.

## Pass 11 — Operational systems

Install machinery, utilities, storage, technical infrastructure.

## Pass 12 — Corporate identity

Apply organization-specific architectural language.

## Pass 13 — Historical layering

Show normal operation through crisis and collapse.

## Pass 14 — Environmental narrative

Add physical evidence of the relevant story.

## Pass 15 — Combat

Integrate vanilla-mob spawner encounters.

## Pass 16 — Loot

Connect existing corporate loot tables.

## Pass 17 — Quest proof

Guarantee and appropriately position required evidence.

## Pass 18 — Damage and decay

Apply physically coherent collapse.

## Pass 19 — Microdetail

Perform final visual polish.

## Pass 20 — Validation

Verify gameplay and technical correctness.

# XLVII. Peak-Quality Review Rubric

Score each completed structure out of 100.

## Functional architecture — 20 points

Does the building make sense as the thing it claims to be?

## Massing and exterior quality — 15 points

Does it have a professional silhouette and facade?

## Interior architecture — 15 points

Are rooms believable, varied, and properly detailed?

## Corporate identity — 10 points

Can its owner be inferred without depending entirely upon logos?

## Operational realism — 10 points

Can the player understand how goods, people, information, energy, or materials moved through it?

## Environmental storytelling — 10 points

Does the structure itself communicate its history?

## Exploration and encounter design — 10 points

Is navigation interesting and combat deliberately staged?

## Loot and quest integration — 5 points

Are existing loot systems and guaranteed proof correctly integrated?

## Damage and historical layering — 5 points

Does the ruin show believable stages of failure?

### Approval threshold

A structure should normally score **90/100 or better** for peak-quality approval.

Additionally:

No major category may be effectively absent.

A spectacular facade cannot compensate for a nonsense interior.

A brilliant interior cannot compensate for broken quest proof.

---

# XLVIII. Automatic Rejection Conditions

Reject the quality pass immediately if any of the following remain:

- major empty boxes without purpose;
- meaningless room chains;
- implausibly tiny architecture caused by refusing to rescale;
- uniformly flat facades;
- unsupported huge roof spans;
- arbitrary window placement;
- random decorative clutter masquerading as detail;
- corporate identity represented only by color;
- lore represented only by books;
- loot tables replaced with improvised chest inventories;
- quest proof left to unreliable random chance;
- spawners scattered without encounter logic;
- inaccessible mandatory evidence;
- major dead ends created accidentally;
- obvious schematic clipping;
- broken doors or stairs;
- impossible player circulation;
- worldgen-breaking dimensions;
- structural IDs or quest hooks lost during rebuilding;
- ruin damage that makes the building's original purpose impossible to interpret.

---

# XLIX. Technical Validation

Before marking the schematic complete verify:

- schematic/NBT loads successfully;
- dimensions are valid;
- structure ID remains correct;
- registry references remain correct;
- processors remain correct;
- bounding behavior remains acceptable;
- terrain placement remains viable;
- entrances do not routinely spawn underground or in midair;
- player circulation works;
- quest evidence exists;
- required chest uses the correct loot/evidence system;
- corporate loot references resolve;
- spawner NBT is valid;
- only authorized vanilla mobs are configured;
- no unavoidable progression-breaking explosion risk exists;
- FTB structure/quest detection remains compatible;
- structure-specific proof remains recoverable.

A visual rebuild is not allowed to break progression.

# LI. Final Quality Doctrine

The purpose of this program is to stop treating Old World structures as Minecraft buildings decorated to resemble a story.

They should instead become **pieces of the Old World itself**.

A VCF facility should look as though people actually cultivated food there.

An Atlas facility should look capable of manufacturing something.

A PolyCore building should look capable of testing materials.

A Pleroma depot should look capable of moving thousands of tonnes of freight.

An Aevum hospital should look capable of saving lives.

A Helion facility should look capable of keeping a city powered.

A Blackglass installation should look capable of preserving civilization's information.

An Asterion facility should look capable of building machines destined for orbit.

A Continuity bunker should look as though brilliant, exhausted people realized the world was dying, gathered everything they could carry, reinforced whatever building they could obtain, filled its rooms with evidence from a civilization that refused to see the whole picture, stocked enough food and filters to buy themselves another few months, and continued working because someone had to understand what had happened.

That is the target.

Not more blocks.

Not more noise.

Not more decoration.

**Purpose. Architecture. Infrastructure. History. Gameplay. Human consequence.**

The finished structure should make the player stop for a moment and look around.

At peak quality, the player should understand what a place was before they understand exactly why it died.
