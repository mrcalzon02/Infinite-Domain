## The Pirbright Institute — BBSRC National Virology Centre, Plowright Building

Pirbright describes the Plowright Building as a Category 4 high-containment laboratory designed both to prevent release and to prevent cross-contamination within the facility. Its design reverses the conventional buried “box inside a box”: a central light-filled people space is surrounded by containment zones whose atrium curtain wall forms a sealed boundary.

Architectural implications for OWS-008:

- observation and staff collaboration can occupy a protected central spine rather than entering each test room;
- containment boundaries should be architectural layers, not merely colored doorways;
- repeated suites can face a common observation/decision space while keeping independent service backs;
- the clean public/command face and the technical containment envelope can have visibly different material and massing languages;
- people-centered oversight makes the repeated failure more legible than hiding every laboratory behind opaque walls.

Reference: https://www.pirbright.ac.uk/facilities-and-resources/specialist-science-facilities/bbsrc-national-virology-centre-plowright-building

## NIAID Integrated Research Facility at Fort Detrick

NIH identifies six coordinated laboratory cores at the Integrated Research Facility: clinical, anatomic pathology, cell culture, imaging physics, comparative medicine and aerobiology. The official facility record also identifies wastewater treatment and water storage, treatment and distribution as building operations rather than invisible off-site assumptions.

Architectural implications for OWS-008:

- the investigation requires distinct but connected technical cores instead of one universal “lab” room;
- dirty examination, imaging/observation, controlled challenge and comparative analysis should share custody and data relationships while retaining separate environmental needs;
- wastewater, wash supply and treatment plant must occupy real volume and connect to the rooms they serve;
- specialist work can converge on a common incident decision without forcing every sample or worker through the same corridor;
- command/analysis should monitor an actual experimental chain rather than face decorative consoles.

References:

- https://www.niaid.nih.gov/sites/default/files/IRF%20Strategic%20Plan%202021_2024%20final.pdf

## USDA National Bio and Agro-Defense Facility / Foreign Animal Disease Diagnostic Laboratory

USDA describes NBAF and the Foreign Animal Disease Diagnostic Laboratory as a combined high-consequence agricultural disease research, diagnostic and response capability. The broader National Animal Health Laboratory Network emphasizes rapid investigation, surveillance, documented diagnostic outcomes and communication of those outcomes to decision-makers during disease events.

Architectural implications for OWS-008:

- this is an agricultural emergency-response institution, so secure receipt, accession, diagnostic work and incident decisions must form one chain;
- response command and laboratory operations need a controlled interface where case status can be compared without contaminating the public/administrative route;
- records and retained samples are operational infrastructure, not decorative lore storage;
- receiving and waste thresholds should read as deliberate emergency logistics;
- VCF's agricultural identity should survive in sample custody, bioscience materials and response language even though the intact facility is already under containment protocols.

References:

- https://www.aphis.usda.gov/labs/about-nvsl/faddl
- https://www.aphis.usda.gov/labs/nahln

## CDC/NIH Biosafety in Microbiological and Biomedical Laboratories (BMBL), Sixth Edition

BMBL is a risk-assessment and biosafety practice authority rather than a single architectural precedent. Its current edition explicitly includes inactivation and verification, large-scale biosafety and clinical-laboratory biosafety. The relevant lesson for this fictional facility is that treatment is not equivalent to proof: inactivation methods, verification, access, facility safeguards and operating procedures must agree.

Planning implications for OWS-008:

- treatment cells need downstream verification rather than a single sterilizer prop declaring success;
- independently monitored treatment branches need identifiable controls, sample custody and service access;
- clean and dirty boundaries must follow the actual assessed process rather than a universal color stripe;
- verification records should be produced beside the experimental sequence and preserved in the incident archive;
- exact real-world biosafety level claims are inappropriate for the fictional fungus and are not being assigned here.

Reference: https://www.cdc.gov/labs/bmbl/index.html

The governing spatial relationships are:

## NIH Design Requirements Manual — biomedical research-facility planning

The NIH Design Requirements Manual establishes planning and technical criteria for safe, reliable biomedical facilities. Its useful architectural lesson is that laboratories are systems of repeatable work modules, deliberate support adjacencies and maintainable building services rather than isolated themed rooms.

Architectural implications for OWS-008:

- treatment and verification rooms should share a legible planning/service rhythm while retaining distinct functions;
- analysis, cold storage, wash/decontamination and supplies belong adjacent to the work they support;
- service distribution and maintenance access must be planned with the laboratory, not added as roof decoration;
- room boundaries, penetrations and mechanical zones must correspond to the pressure/cleanliness sequence;
- command and records spaces should monitor the operation without becoming part of the specimen route.

Reference: https://orf.od.nih.gov/TechnicalResources/Pages/DesignRequirementsManual2016.aspx

## CDC/NIH BMBL — controlled access, sealed boundaries and directional airflow

The sixth-edition *Biosafety in Microbiological and Biomedical Laboratories* uses protocol-driven risk assessment and identifies facility design as one part of containment. Its laboratory guidance emphasizes controlled separation from unrestricted traffic, sequential doors/access boundaries, cleanable surfaces, sealable penetrations, handwashing near exits, decontamination capability and directional exhaust kept away from occupied areas and air intakes.

Architectural implications for OWS-008:

- the public intake cannot open directly into the validation suite;
- staff and materials need explicit sequential thresholds rather than decorative yellow floors;
- concealed penetrations are both a credible engineering concern and the target's narrative focus;
- each treatment/hold room needs a visible relation to supply, exhaust and monitoring;
- waste should be decontaminated before crossing back toward ordinary service circulation;
- air intakes and exhaust stacks must be separated in the roof/site composition.

Reference: https://www.cdc.gov/labs/bmbl/index.html

## USDA ARS Facilities Design Standards — clean/biohazard change and service barriers

USDA ARS containment design is particularly relevant to a plant/fungal investigation facility. Its terminology and design material distinguish clean and biohazard change spaces, airlocks, sealed construction penetrations, specialized air handling and waste management. The larger lesson is that the containment barrier includes people, utilities, drains and service connections—not only the test room walls.

Architectural implications for OWS-008:

- clean entry and dirty exit functions should be spatially legible and should not collapse into one lobby;
- service penetrations need accessible inspection faces and multiple visible seal generations;
- the hidden joint/service spine should connect vertically to roof plant and downward to drainage/effluent control;
- containment support volume must be substantial relative to test-room volume;
- contaminated materials need a removal path sized and controlled independently from staff circulation.

Reference: https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/FD/CIAMMB/242.1M%20ARS%20Facilities%20Design%20Standards.pdf

## WHO decontamination and waste-management guidance — treatment choice and full waste chain

The WHO Laboratory Biosafety Manual's decontamination and waste-management monograph treats decontamination as a risk-informed process covering selection, handling, transport, treatment and final disposal rather than a single magical machine. That supports an OWS-008 program where different treatment methods are compared and where failed procedure changes remain traceable.

Architectural implications for OWS-008:

- different treatment rooms should visibly serve different material/process constraints;
- dirty holding, treatment, verification and treated/clean holding must be separate stages;
- containers, tools and waste require controlled transfer points and temporary storage;
- monitoring and records belong next to the process because validation is as important as treatment;
- the waste route must continue to a real hold/removal threshold rather than end at a decorative barrel.

Reference: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240011359
