## Research objective

The goal is not to find a real building identical to OWS-002. The goal is to establish credible spatial relationships for four things that the structure must combine:

1. temporary conversion of an existing community facility for emergency use;
2. controlled-environment crop production inside a non-purpose-built civic shell;
3. public emergency registration / handover / distribution;
4. disciplined receiving, storage, exception handling, packing and dispatch.

The sources below are used as functional precedents. They do not dictate literal visual copying.

## Precedent A — FEMA existing-facility emergency conversion

**Source:** FEMA Resource Typing Library Tool, Short-Term Shelter / Evacuation Shelter definitions.  
**References:**  
https://rtlt.preptoolkit.fema.gov/Public/Resource/View/9-508-1197  
https://rtlt.preptoolkit.fema.gov/Public/Resource/View/9-508-1192

FEMA explicitly defines emergency shelters as **existing facilities such as schools, community centers, convention centers or churches temporarily converted** to meet disaster-survivor needs. The typed functions include feeding, hydration, sanitation and other life-sustaining services.

### Architectural lesson

Emergency infrastructure does not need to erase the previous building identity. A believable conversion often works by overlaying temporary but organized systems onto familiar civic architecture.

### OWS-002 translation

- Preserve the community-center silhouette and civic front door.
- Make the conversion obvious through routing, controls, production equipment and relief functions rather than by replacing the shell with an industrial warehouse.
- Keep public sanitation/support relationships plausible.
- Allow traces of former civic rooms to remain visible beneath the emergency program.

## Precedent B — Logistics Cluster distribution-site layout

**Source:** Logistics Operational Guide, `Before Distribution`.  
**Reference:** https://log.logcluster.org/en/distribution-0

The guidance calls for distribution sites to provide distinct entry and exit points, waiting areas, registration, handover, storage, staff facilities and population support. The important point is that public distribution is a **sequence of controlled spaces**, not a pile of goods in an open room.

### Architectural lesson

Crowd-facing relief work requires controlled throughput. Waiting, registration, handover and departure should be physically legible, and storage should sit behind the handover point rather than forcing recipients through logistics space.

### OWS-002 translation

- The north civic approach becomes a queue/waiting zone.
- Registration and emergency authorization occupy the front administrative threshold.
- Culture/food handover is adjacent but controlled.
- Public departure should not require reversing through the entire queue.
- Relief stock sits behind the issue point or moves through a separate staff route.
- The south/east hall exits can become controlled outbound/service thresholds rather than generic fire doors.

## Precedent C — Logistics Cluster receiving, inspection and dispatch flow

**Source:** Logistics Operational Guide, `Goods Flow Management`.  
**Reference:** https://log.logcluster.org/en/goods-flow-management

The guidance separates cargo reception from dispatch. At reception, incoming goods are counted, inspected, recorded and assigned to appropriate storage; damaged or expired goods are separated into a clearly marked location. Dispatch is planned, picked, consolidated and staged before release.

### Architectural lesson

Receiving, exception/hold, storage and dispatch are different states and should occupy different positions in the building. A questionable batch should interrupt the normal flow at a real inspection/decision point.

### OWS-002 translation

- Incoming culture/nutrient stock enters on the service side.
- A batch/condition check precedes clean stock.
- The later D1 anomaly uses an ordinary marked hold/isolation position rather than a horror-room invention.
- Packing and pallet staging sit immediately upstream of outbound relief dispatch.
- Records/authorization are tied to allocation and stock decisions.

## Precedent D — USDA controlled-environment agriculture system requirements

**Source:** USDA National Agricultural Library controlled-environment agriculture research; USDA NIFA/ERS controlled-environment agriculture material.  
**References:**  
https://www.nal.usda.gov/research-tools/food-safety-research-projects/characterization-and-control-food-safety-risks-associated-indoor-leafy-green-growing-and-harvest  
https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/109422  
https://portal.nifa.usda.gov/enterprise-search/projects/1025487

USDA materials describe controlled-environment agriculture as systems managing environmental factors such as lighting, water, ventilation and nutrient delivery. Indoor/vertical facilities commonly rely on nutrient solutions, growth substrates and LED lighting; vertical use of indoor volume is a deliberate production strategy rather than simply stacking plants decoratively.

### Architectural lesson

A grow hall needs visible environmental-service logic. Crop racks are only one component of a system that also needs water/nutrient delivery, lighting/daylight, maintenance access, air movement and harvest access.

### OWS-002 translation

- Preserve the roof lantern as useful daylight architecture.
- Give the hall repeated cultivation bays rather than three decorative crop strips.
- Maintain crop-service aisles wide enough for staff and harvest movement.
- Run irrigation/nutrient distribution as a service network with pumps/manifold/control rather than one pipe crossing the room.
- Keep maintenance access to overhead/roof systems.
- Do not over-industrialize the civic shell; the emergency system should look modular and rapidly installed.

## Precedent E — Penn State hydroponic nutrient-service practice

**Source:** Penn State Extension, `Hydroponics Systems: Nutrient Solution Programs and Recipes`.  
**Reference:** https://extension.psu.edu/hydroponics-systems-nutrient-solution-programs-and-recipes

The extension guidance describes concentrated nutrient solutions, stock tanks, separation of incompatible concentrates, agitation/circulation pumps and material-compatibility concerns.

### Architectural lesson

Nutrient support needs an actual utility position. It should not be implied by a single decorative pipe.

### OWS-002 translation

- Dedicate a staff-only nutrient/service bay adjacent to the cultivation hall.
- Use at least paired storage/make-up positions rather than one anonymous tank if final block palette allows.
- Connect pumps/manifolds visibly to the grow rows.
- Keep the service bay out of the public route.

## Precedent F — Emergency food-storage / warehouse zoning

**Source:** Logistics Cluster, `Emergency Food Storage Management` and `Warehouse Space and Layout`.  
**References:**  
https://logcluster.org/en/documents/indeci-presentation-emergency-food-storage-management  
https://logcluster.org/en/document/warehouse-space-and-layout

Emergency food logistics guidance emphasizes location, area use, security, equipment, receiving, storage and dispatch. The Logistics Cluster's warehouse training also distinguishes goods receipt, storage, picking, repacking damaged items and dispatch.

### Architectural lesson

Even a temporary relief operation becomes spatially organized when throughput matters. Overflow should accumulate at real choke points such as staging/dispatch, not in arbitrary corners.

### OWS-002 translation

- The yellow overflow field belongs at dispatch/staging.
- Pallets/crates accumulate near a real exit/loading handoff.
- Clean incoming stock is separated from outbound packed relief goods.
- Exception stock is isolated near inspection rather than mixed into clean storage.

## Combined design rules derived from precedent

1. **Adaptive reuse remains visible.** The final building should still read as a community center converted under emergency authority.
2. **Public distribution is a sequence.** Waiting, registration, authorization, handover and departure must be spatially legible.
3. **Back-of-house agriculture is a production system.** Crop racks require nutrient, irrigation, daylight/lighting, maintenance and harvest support.
4. **Receiving precedes clean storage.** Incoming cultures and nutrient stock need inspection/batch handling before entering the grow system.
5. **Exception handling is ordinary infrastructure.** D1 isolation belongs at a real service/inspection node.
6. **Packing and dispatch are separate from cultivation.** Harvest must leave the grow hall through wash/check and packing before becoming staged relief stock.
7. **Overflow pressure should appear at throughput choke points.** Do not scatter relief crates randomly.
8. **The civic shell should become more heterogeneous, not less.** Temporary agricultural/logistics insertions should visibly coexist with retained masonry, windows, roof lantern and public-room proportions.
