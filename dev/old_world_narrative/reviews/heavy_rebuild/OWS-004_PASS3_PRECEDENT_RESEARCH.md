## Cornell Small Farms — indoor mushroom production

Cornell's indoor-production guidance describes mushroom cultivation as controlled-environment agriculture in which separate chambers manage temperature, humidity, light and airflow. It distinguishes an incubation stage from a later fruiting stage, with material moved to a different environment when growth conditions change. Cornell also emphasizes sanitation and cross-contamination control as advantages/requirements of indoor systems.

Architectural implications for OWS-004:

- a cultivation floor should not be one undifferentiated open office slab;
- environmental zones and service aisles must be physically legible;
- air handling, humidity and temperature control deserve visible mechanical infrastructure;
- transfer between growth stages should use controlled material circulation rather than decorative crop rows;
- clean/service boundaries are part of production architecture.

Reference: https://smallfarms.cornell.edu/resources/indoor-production/

## Cornell Small Farms — seven stages / fruiting environmental control

Cornell's commercial cultivation material outlines stages including strain selection, substrate preparation, inoculation, incubation, initiation, fruiting and harvest. Its fruiting guidance identifies temperature, humidity, lighting and oxygen/fresh-air exchange as core controlled variables and notes that dense mushroom rooms require deliberate intake/exhaust ventilation.

Architectural implications:

- the tower requires a substantial environmental-service spine and roof plant;
- cultivation floors need supply/return logic, not merely water tanks;
- repeated floors may represent controlled production modules with different crop stages/batches rather than identical decorative rooms;
- roof intake/exhaust and service risers can become major exterior identity elements;
- later containment can plausibly isolate an environmental branch on one upper floor while lower branches remain active.

Reference: https://smallfarms.cornell.edu/resources/methods-of-commercial-mushroom-cultivation-in-the-northeastern-united-states/2-seven-stages-of-cultivation/

## Penn State Extension — commercial mushroom production sequence

Penn State describes commercial production through substrate/compost preparation, spawning, casing, pinning and cropping, followed by post-crop pasteurization/steam-off. It emphasizes management of starting ingredients, sanitary conditions, fresh-air/CO2 control and removal/treatment of spent substrate to prevent contamination of later crops.

Architectural implications:

- clean incoming culture/substrate and spent/dirty returns should not share an unexplained route;
- harvest is not the final material movement: spent biological material and sanitation/service return need a plausible path;
- floor modules need inspection/sanitation capacity;
- a service/freight core should connect production to the industrial base;
- the tower's active-containment history should build on already-existing isolation and sanitation systems rather than introduce arbitrary quarantine walls.

Reference: https://extension.psu.edu/forage-and-food-crops/mushrooms/production-and-harvesting

## Public-facing urban CEA lesson

Cornell notes that indoor mushroom cultivation can be adapted to urban and reused structures because the environmental conditions are created mechanically rather than supplied by open farmland. OWS-004 pushes that principle into the setting's advanced pre-collapse economy: a dense urban tower publicly demonstrates that controlled fungal agriculture can decouple production from traditional land area and weather.

Architectural implication:

The public demonstration podium should be a deliberate narrative contrast to the controlled industrial tower above it. Visitors see a clean, optimistic VCF technology showcase while freight, sanitation, substrate returns and environmental plant remain on separate service routes.

## Precedent synthesis for Minecraft

The relevant real-world lessons are not exact room dimensions. They are relationships:

- **controlled chambers** instead of open generic floors;
- **environmental plant + vertical distribution** instead of isolated props;
- **clean material in / harvest out / spent material return** as distinct flows;
- **sanitation and contamination control** built into circulation;
- **repeated monitored production modules** connected to a service core;
- **public viewing separated from production access**;
- **branchable environmental services** that make later single-floor containment believable.

These principles will govern Pass 4 adjacency and Gate-B intact architecture.
